    Standard Tank Flushing in Cape Girardeau

    Flushing is the one job every tank needs, gas or electric, new or old. Hard water makes it more urgent. We drain, partly refill and drain again until the hose runs clear, sometimes four or five rounds. Then we close the valve and check that it reseals without a drip.

    Anode Upgrades for Odor in Cape Girardeau, MO

    A rotten egg smell from the hot side only is almost always sulfate-reducing bacteria reacting with a magnesium anode. Swapping the rod usually fixes it. We replace it with an aluminum-zinc rod or a powered anode, then chlorinate the tank to kill the bacteria living in it. Powered anodes never wear out and draw very little electricity.

    Sediment Flush and Descaling in Cape Girardeau

    Calcium carbonate drops out of hard water every time the tank heats, and over a year it can harden into chunks the size of a thumbnail. Those chunks cause the popping. For stubborn scale we use a potable-rated descaler, then flush it clean. A clean bottom puts less heat stress on the seam.

    T&P Valve Replacement in Cape Girardeau

    Replacing a relief valve is a short job, but it has to be done right. The new valve must match the tank's BTU or kilowatt rating. We drain the tank below the valve opening, thread in the new one with pipe dope rated for potable water, and reconnect a discharge pipe in copper, CPVC or another approved material. A capped discharge pipe is a serious hazard.

    Pricing for a standard tank visit usually runs $100 to $250, and that covers the flush, the anode inspection and the relief valve test on a typical 40 or 50 gallon tank. Attic tanks can cost a bit more. Larger commercial units get their own quote.

    Once a year is the baseline almost everywhere. On hard water, go every six months. Homes with a softener and light use can sometimes stretch it, but we still want to see the anode every year.

    All the major ones, including Rheem, Ruud, AO Smith, Bradford White, State, American and Kenmore-branded tanks. We can source parts for each. Store-brand tanks are usually made by the same few factories.

    Many manufacturers write regular maintenance into the warranty terms and can deny a claim for sediment damage. Read the fine print on yours. A dated invoice that shows a flush and anode check each year is the simplest proof, and we leave one after every visit.

    It is a long rod of magnesium or aluminum screwed into the top of the tank. It corrodes so the tank does not. Once it is used up, the water starts attacking the glass lining and the steel behind it, and a tank with a spent anode can rust through in a few years.

    A DIY flush clears loose sediment and stops there. We pull and measure the anode. We also lift the relief valve, gauge the expansion tank and house pressure, check draft or element resistance, and look at every fitting for corrosion, which is where most early warnings show up.

    Rumbling is the deeper cousin of popping, and it means a thick bed of sediment is shifting and boiling on the bottom of the tank each time the burner lights. It gets louder over time. Electric tanks rumble less, but they crust up the lower element instead.

    Three to five years for a magnesium rod is typical on hard water. Softened water can cut that in half. We measure the rod at each visit and tell you roughly how many years are left, so you replace it before the lining starts to rust rather than after.
