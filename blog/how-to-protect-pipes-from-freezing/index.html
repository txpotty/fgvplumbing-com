    How to Protect Your Pipes Before the Next Missouri Freeze

    By Andrew Blake · October 6, 2026 · 5 min read

    Pipes in exterior walls, crawlspaces and unheated garages are the most common trouble spots when temperatures drop sharply.

    Pipes do not need a long winter to burst. Even mild-weather towns get the occasional night that sits in the low twenties from midnight to sunrise. That is when houses built without freeze in mind get caught. Supply lines run in exterior walls, the backflow preventer sits bare in the yard, and the pool pump has no cover at all. A single cold night can split a line that never gave you trouble before.

    Why Frozen Pipes Burst

    Water expands about nine percent as it freezes. Oddly enough, the ice is not what does the damage. When the plug forms, it shoves the water ahead of it toward a closed faucet or valve, and pressure builds in that trapped section until the copper splits or a brass valve body cracks. While the line is still frozen, the crack stays plugged with ice. Most people find the damage around ten in the morning, once the sun thaws the line and it starts spraying.

    Above-ground pipe is the weak spot in most houses. Bare copper and PVC lose heat fast on a clear night once the sun goes down. Wind across an open yard pulls heat away even faster. A backflow preventer sitting in the open on a windy night can freeze solid at temperatures that a pipe inside a wall would never notice.

    The Parts of a House Most at Risk

    Before the forecast drops, take a lap around the house with a flashlight and check these spots:

    • Backflow preventers on irrigation and fire lines. The brass body sits above ground and costs real money to replace when it splits.
    • Hose bibs on exterior walls, particularly the old compression style that is not frost-free.
    • Pool and spa equipment, meaning the pump, filter, heater and the PVC between them.
    • Irrigation risers and drip manifolds sitting exposed in the beds.
    • Evaporative cooler supply lines that climb the outside wall to the roof, on houses that have them.
    • Water heaters and supply pipe in unheated garages, mostly in colder areas.
    • The main supply where it comes up out of the ground near the meter or pressure regulator.

    Cheap Steps That Prevent Damage

    Most freeze protection costs less than a service call. Slide foam pipe sleeves over every exposed run and tape the seams so cold air cannot get in. Pull the hose off each hose bib and fit an insulated cover, because a connected hose traps water in the faucet body. For a backflow preventer, use an insulated pouch, or wrap it in old towels and set a bucket over it. Low-wattage heat tape rated for outdoor plumbing is money well spent where hard freezes are common.

    Check the pool controller for a freeze protection setting. Most modern controllers run the pump automatically when the air gets near 34 or 35 degrees. During a hard freeze, let a faucet on an outside wall drip overnight, which relieves pressure and keeps water moving. Open the sink cabinets on exterior walls. Warm room air needs a path to the pipes.

    Water heaters face heavy demand in winter, when incoming water is cold and families use more hot water.

    Colder climates call for permanent fixes. A frost-free sillcock moves the shutoff 6 to 12 inches inside the house, where the wall stays warm. Insulated crawlspaces and heat tape on the runs that froze last year handle most of what is left. If you leave a cabin or second home empty for the winter, shut off the main, open the low drains and blow the lines out, since a pipe with no water in it cannot split.

    If a Pipe Freezes

    A trickle from the kitchen faucet on a cold morning usually means a frozen line. Leave that faucet open. Work along the pipe with a hair dryer, starting near the faucet and moving toward the cold section, or set a space heater a safe distance away. Never use a torch or any open flame. Have the main shutoff located first, because a split pipe leaks the minute the ice lets go. A split hose bib or one section of pipe usually runs $200 to $500 to repair, and a cracked backflow preventer can be $400 to $900 or more installed.

    When to Call a Professional

    Some freeze damage needs a licensed plumber right away. That means a split pipe or a cracked backflow body, water coming through a ceiling, or a frozen line you cannot reach safely.
