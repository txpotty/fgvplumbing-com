    Tankless vs. Tank Water Heaters: What Works Best in Missouri

    By Travis Baines · September 18, 2026 · 5 min read

    How a Tank Water Heater Works

    A standard tank heater keeps 40 or 50 gallons hot around the clock. The design is simple, replacement is cheap, and a swap usually takes a few hours. The downside is standby loss. The tank loses heat through its walls all day, which means the heater fires even when no one is running water.

    Groundwater from limestone areas is especially hard, which makes softeners and regular water heater flushing worthwhile.

    Tanks typically last 8 to 12 years. Inside the tank, an anode rod corrodes so the steel does not, and when the rod is used up the tank begins to rust. Replacing the anode every few years can add years of life. Few homeowners ever do it.

    How Tankless Units Work

    A tankless heater fires only on demand. It heats water as it flows through a heat exchanger, so nothing sits in storage losing heat. You never run out, which is the main reason people switch. The catch is flow rate, because each unit can only heat so many gallons per minute at once.

    Incoming water temperature sets how much hot water a tankless unit can deliver. A unit rated at 9 gallons per minute might only manage 5 or 6 in winter, when the water coming in is much colder. That is enough for one shower and a dishwasher, but maybe not two showers and a washer. Size for winter.

    Cost and Installation

    Swapping a tank is mostly a like-for-like job. Tankless installs usually cost more because the gas line and venting both change, and sometimes the electrical does too. A typical gas tankless unit draws 150,000 to 199,000 BTUs, more than many half-inch gas lines can carry. Upsizing to 3/4 inch is common.

    Venting changes too. A condensing unit vents through PVC, and a non-condensing one needs stainless vent. Our installers at FGV Plumbing check the gas line size and the vent route before we quote anything, so there are no surprises on install day. A condensing unit also needs a condensate drain, often through a neutralizer cartridge.

    Many communities use a version of the International or Uniform Plumbing Code, and plumber licensing is handled locally.

    Either type needs a temperature and pressure relief valve piped to a safe spot. With a pressure reducing valve on the main, a tank heater usually needs a thermal expansion tank to go with it. Permits are standard for both kinds of installs. We pull them.

    Which One Fits Your Missouri Home

    A tank still makes sense for plenty of houses. In a two-bath house where budget matters most, a same-size replacement tank is usually the easy call. Tankless earns its cost in houses that already run out of hot water, or where the tank takes up a closet you would like to reclaim. Space is a real factor.

    Tankless units often run 20 years with yearly maintenance, about twice what a tank lasts. With hard water, plan on a yearly flush of the heat exchanger using a small pump and descaling solution. Without it, expect error codes within a couple of years. Gas units also need a clean air intake.

    Getting a Straight Answer

    Bring us the age of your current heater, the number of people in the house and whether anyone has ever run out of hot water mid-shower.
