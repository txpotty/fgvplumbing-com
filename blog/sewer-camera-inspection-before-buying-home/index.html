    Why a Sewer Camera Inspection Belongs on Every Missouri Home Inspection

    By Andrew Blake · August 13, 2026 · 5 min read

    A general home inspection covers a lot. The inspector checks the roof, the electrical panel, the HVAC and the water heater, along with whatever plumbing is exposed. What most of them do not do is look inside the main sewer line that runs from the house to the street or septic tank. Running water in every fixture for 20 minutes proves very little, because a cracked or root-packed line can drain fine during a short test. For a buyer, a sewer scope is one of the most useful add-ons to order before closing.

    What a Sewer Camera Inspection Involves

    The plumber feeds a waterproof camera on a flexible push cable through a cleanout, usually near the foundation or out in the front yard. The video records as the camera moves down the pipe, and you can watch it live on the monitor. A transmitter at the camera head lets us mark the spot and depth of any problem from the surface. Plan on 30 to 60 minutes. Ask for the video file and a written summary before the plumber leaves.

    No cleanout? We can sometimes go in from a roof vent or through a toilet flange. That access may add to the price. Most residential scopes run $150 to $350 when there is a cleanout to work from.

    Before the visit, ask the listing agent for any records of past sewer work. Root cutting invoices or an old lining warranty are worth having. That history tells the plumber what to look for and whether earlier repairs are holding up. Sellers often have it in a drawer.

    Ask the seller, too, whether the house has ever had a main line backup into the basement or the lowest shower. A yes there tells us to look hard at the first 20 feet out from the foundation. Honest answers save time.

    What the Camera Can Reveal

    Sewer problems are expensive, and you rarely see them from inside the house. A camera can turn up:

    • Root intrusion at the joints, most often near big, older trees.
    • Bellies or sags where the pipe settled and waste collects.
    • Offset or separated joints from years of soil movement.
    • Cracked or collapsed sections in clay tile or cast iron.
    • Heavy corrosion and scale inside older cast iron.
    • Grease buildup that points to ongoing maintenance.
    • Bad past repairs, such as mismatched pipe joined without proper couplings.

    Why It Matters for Missouri Buyers

    Older brick homes in Missouri often still have galvanized supply pipe and cast iron drains, which corrode and narrow over time.

    Every era of construction brings its own sewer risks. In older parts of town, the original clay or cast iron is often still in service decades past its expected life. A lot of houses from the 1960s through the 1980s have cast iron under the slab that rots from the inside. Newer ABS and PVC is not immune either. Bad bedding and clay that moves can still sag the line or pull a joint apart.

    Trees add another wrinkle. Buyers love mature shade trees, and so do sewer lines, because the roots follow moisture straight to the joints. Rural lots and some edge-of-town properties use septic instead. There the camera shows the condition of the line out to the tank, and that footage goes nicely with a separate septic inspection.

    Regular pumping and inspection keep septic systems working properly, and well water should be tested on a regular schedule.

    How the Results Help You Negotiate

    Sewer repairs cost real money. Figure $1,500 to $4,500 for a spot repair, and $4,000 to $12,000 or more for a new lateral or a trenchless liner. Finding a problem before closing gives you options. The seller can make the repair or give you a credit, or you can decide the house is not the one. If the line looks good, you get a baseline video to compare against later. A seller who scopes before listing gets to handle any repair on their own timeline, not in the middle of a tense negotiation.

    When to Call a Professional

    Book the scope early in your inspection contingency period. You want room on the calendar for repair quotes in case the footage turns up a problem. Hire a licensed plumber rather than a general inspector, since a plumber can read the footage and price the real repair.
